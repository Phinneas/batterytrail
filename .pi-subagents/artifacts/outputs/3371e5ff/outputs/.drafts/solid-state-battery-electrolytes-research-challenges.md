## Challenge-by-challenge findings

### 1. Solid–solid interfacial contact: voids, contact loss, interfacial resistance growth, composite cathode design

- **Void formation during stripping is a first-order failure mode.** When Li is stripped from the Li|SE interface faster than it can be replenished (via diffusion/creep), vacancies accumulate and nucleate voids; voids grow, shrink, and partially replenish on redeposition (operando SEM at Li|LLZO) [12]. Kasemchainan et al. identified a "critical stripping current": exceeding it causes void accumulation that later seeds dendrites on plating and cell failure [4]. This voids→dendrite link is now standard in the field [1][4][10][20].
- **Consequence: interfacial resistance growth and contact loss.** Voids reduce true contact area → current focusing, higher local current density, and rising interfacial resistance; operando X-ray tomography shows void + interphase evolution jointly degrade electrochemistry [10]. Interfacial (charge-transfer) resistance is a dominant contributor to total cell resistance in ceramic cells, and air-exposed LLZO surfaces (Li2CO3/LiOH from CO2/H2O) inflate it further [31].
- **Stack pressure is the standard mitigation but is not free.** Pressure is typically applied during stripping to drive Li creep and suppress voids [51]; 2–13 MPa was applied in Li|LLZO symmetric-cell studies to maintain conformal contact [11]. Whether pressure is truly required is debated: QuantumScape reports cycling with zero external pressure in its anode-free cells [45][46], and a free-standing thin-film LiPON design cycles Li metal with no external pressure [53] — but both rely on special architectures (thin separators, in-situ formed Li), which is the exception, not the norm [45]. High pressure is incompatible with standard pouch formats and adds pack mass/complexity [14][49].
- **Cathode–electrolyte composite design is a transport bottleneck.** Fast SE conductivity alone is insufficient: composite cathodes need percolating ionic+electronic networks with SE volume fractions ≤30% for high energy density, and at such low fractions tortuosity/poor contacts dominate, limiting power [50]. Janek & Zeier's 2023 review identifies composite-cathode transport as a central unresolved challenge, requiring SEs with conductivities ≳10 mS cm⁻¹ for thick electrodes [15]. Most lab cells still use ~500 µm pressed SE pellets — far from commercial form factors [49].
- **Confidence:** high for void mechanism (multiple primary sources read); medium for pressure-necessity debate (self-reported company claims).

### 2. Lithium dendrite penetration: short-circuit mechanisms, CCD, and the "dendrite-free" debate

- **"Dendrite-free" ceramic claims do not hold.** Hatzell et al. (2020): the hypothesis that solid electrolytes are impermeable to Li "has been disproven under a variety of electrolyte compositions and cycling conditions" [9]. Even dense, single-crystal-grade LLZO fails: Han et al. (2019) showed Li filaments nucleate *inside* the electrolyte because of local/electronic conductivity at grain boundaries — penetration is not purely a mechanical crack problem [3].
- **Mechanism debate: grain boundaries vs pores.** Two camps historically: (a) propagation along grain boundaries / softened grain boundaries / microcracks [2][8][3], and (b) initiation at (subsurface) pores/defects, which are unavoidable in sintered ceramics. The 2023 Nature study (Ning et al.) largely reconciles them: **initiation** = Li plating into subsurface pores connected to the surface by microcracks, with pressure buildup cracking the ceramic; **propagation** = wedge-opening, with Li filling a dry crack from the rear (not the tip) — grain-boundary fracture strength controls initiation, while macroscopic fracture toughness, filament length, current density, stack pressure and cycled capacity control propagation [1]. Lower stack pressure *suppresses propagation* — the opposite of the naive "more pressure = better" picture for plating failure [1].
- **Critical current density (CCD) is the key metric — and it is not standardized.** CCD >3 mA cm⁻² is the oft-cited bar for EV-relevant power, "not yet achieved" for LLZO per Krauskopf et al. (2020), who argue for a "natural" CCD limit set by Li transport [6]. Reported CCDs vary by orders of magnitude depending on protocol: e.g., ~50 µA cm⁻² at 0.09 MPa stack pressure in one LLZO study [52], vs multi-mA cm⁻² in optimized cells; the community has called for standardized CCD measurement (Communications Chemistry comment) [7]. Areal capacity, not just current density, determines short-circuiting in sulfide cells (Lewis et al. 2022, cited in [15]). "Dendrite-free" or high-CCD headline claims must therefore be treated with skepticism unless protocol details are given [7][15].
- **Confidence:** high (multiple primary sources read directly).

### 3. Electrochemical stability windows vs Li metal and high-voltage cathodes; coating strategies

- **Thermodynamic windows are narrow — CV experiments overstate them.** DFT decomposition windows (V vs Li/Li⁺): LPS 1.72–2.30; LPSB 1.72–2.01; LPSC (argyrodite) 1.72–2.01; LGPS 1.63–2.14; halide Li3YBr6 0.67–3.06; LiPON 0.68–2.64; LLZO 0.05–2.68; LLTO 1.71–3.36 (Table 2 of JACS Au 2021) [32]. So sulfides are reductively unstable vs Li metal (decompose below ~1.7 V, P⁵⁺→P⁰→Li3P) and oxidatively unstable above ~2.0–2.5 V (S²⁻→S⁰) — i.e., unstable against both Li metal and standard NMC cathodes; oxides (LLZO, LATP) are oxidatively stable but LLZO's window still starts near 0 V vs Li [32]. Han et al. (2016) showed LGPS reversibly reduces/oxidizes at 0–1.7 V and 2–2.5 V — far narrower than the 0–5 V suggested by semi-blocking CV, explaining "hidden" interfacial decomposition that raises resistance [33]. Kinetically, "intrinsic" windows are wider than thermodynamic ones (metastable (de)lithiated phases), which is why CV overestimates stability [32].
- **Practical consequences:** sulfide SEs require protective interlayers at both electrodes — alloy anodes (Si, In, Li-Si) instead of bare Li [15], and oxidation-blocking cathode coatings; halides sit between (good oxidation stability, but halides like Li3YBr6 are reduced near 0.67 V, i.e., not stable vs Li metal either — Riegger et al. 2021, cited in [15]).
- **Coatings (LiNbO3-type) are the standard cathode-side fix.** LiNbO3 (and co-LiNbO3) coatings on NMC prevent direct contact between the sulfide SE and the oxidizing cathode: demonstrated for NCM622|Li6PS5Cl at 4.3 V [34]; coating quality/route matters and thick coatings introduce their own unfavorable behaviors at ≥4.4 V [35]. Alternatives include Li3InCl6-type halides with better oxidative stability and air tolerance [30].
- **Confidence:** high for window numbers (table read directly); medium for coating efficacy claims (abstract-level, one PDF blocked).

### 4. Moisture/air sensitivity: H2S from sulfides, dry-room cost; halide vs oxide tolerance

- **Sulfides react with water to evolve toxic H2S** plus solid byproducts (Li3PO4, LiOH) that raise interfacial impedance [22][23]. This is a materials-level showstopper for manufacturing: gloveboxes (<1 ppm H2O) are fine for lab, but production-scale dry rooms are required [22][25], with occupational health & safety measures for H2S exposure [24]. Dry-room requirements exceed Li-ion: Li-ion production runs ~−32 °C dew point, while sulfide-based SSB processes need <−45 °C, with dehumidification airflow demand 2–5× higher in the same floor space [26]. Sulfides' moisture hypersensitivity is "incompatible with today's lithium-ion battery manufacturing infrastructure" (Nat. Commun. 2024) [27]; that paper's reversible surface modification is a mitigation research line [27].
- **Oxides are more tolerant but not inert:** LLZO degrades in air via CO2/H2O reactions (Li⁺/H⁺ exchange, Li2CO3 formation) that increase interfacial resistance [31]; so oxide processing also needs atmosphere control, just less extreme.
- **Halides are intermediate:** chloride SSEs are "more balanced" (conductivity, stability, moisture resistance) [29]; dry-room compatibility assessments exist [28], and Li3InCl6 is claimed air-stable and even water-synthesizable [30]. However, "inherent air instability of halide SSEs remains a critical barrier" for some chemistries (e.g., Li3YCl6 needs coatings for moisture stability) — so halide tolerance is material-specific, not universal (RSC Chem. Commun. 2025, cited via [29]-adjacent search).
- **Confidence:** high (multiple primary sources; dry-room numbers from trade press [26], medium).

### 5. Mechanical properties and stack pressure requirements

- **Sulfide SEs are ductile/soft (low Young's modulus) → good cold-press processability** but deform under pressure; oxides (LLZO, LATP) are brittle ceramics that crack/fracture, especially on cycling and at grain boundaries [15][19][1]. LLZO's brittleness and the difficulty of fabricating defect-free thin sheets are explicitly flagged as commercialization barriers [19].
- **Stack pressure is required for most inorganic all-solid-state cells** to maintain solid–solid contact during cycling — unlike liquid cells [13][14][51]. Representative numbers found:
  - LLZO symmetric cells: 2–13 MPa applied to maintain conformal contact [11];
  - Low-pressure extreme: 0.09 MPa → CCD collapses to ~50 µA cm⁻² [52];
  - Columnar-Si | sulfide cells: 25 MPa applied to compensate anode expansion [48];
  - QuantumScape: ≤3.4 atm (~0.34 MPa) in its "gold-standard" test conditions, and claims of zero external pressure in coin cells [45][46];
  - Thin-film LiPON: no external pressure [53].
  So the field spans ~0 (thin-film/engineered interfaces) to 25 MPa (volume-changing alloy anodes), with 1–13 MPa typical for lab LLZO/sulfide press cells; pack-level pressure adds structural mass and complicates cell formats [14][49]. The Nature Reviews Materials review (2024) treats external pressure as a coupled design variable for both fabrication and operation [14].
- **Important tension:** for dendrite *propagation*, lower stack pressure is better [1]; for contact maintenance and stripping (voids), higher pressure is better [51][11] — the "stack pressure dilemma" [14]. Practical cells must find an intermediate window (or eliminate the need for pressure via architecture, e.g., thin separators [53][45]).
- **Confidence:** high (numbers each attributed; range across architectures is real).

### 6. Manufacturing / scale-up economics

- **Sintering temperature:** dense LLZO requires high-temperature sintering >1200 °C [36][37] (some processes ~1100–1230 °C; sintering aids and reactive sintering lower it [37], and ultrafast sintering is being developed). High-temperature co-sintering with cathodes risks interdiffusion and Li loss (Li sublimation) [37].
- **Material cost:** initial LLZ price ~US$2000/kg (MSE Supplies quote, >100 kg) dominates oxide-battery material cost at early scale, expected to fall with volume [38]. Li metal anode cost target: ~US$2.08 m⁻² to match graphite cost in a 5.4 mAh cm⁻² / 3.8 V cell [17]; thin-Li fabrication (e.g., thermal evaporation) drives cost and scrap [17]. Cathode active materials are interchangeable with Li-ion (no cost delta), but SE layers, dry rooms, and new cell assembly add cost [17][15].
- **Manufacturing infrastructure:** sulfide production cannot reuse standard Li-ion dry rooms (dew point −45 °C vs −32 °C; 2–5× dehumidification) [26]; the entire cell-format question is unresolved — press/pellet cells (500 µm separators) dominate literature but are not commercial form factors [49]; pouch cells with thin (≤50 µm) separators are the industry goal (T3's track covers company specifics).
- **Cost benchmarks vs incumbent:** Li-ion pack $108/kWh (2025), cell $74/kWh; McKinsey projects NMC packs $75–90/kWh and LFP $55–65/kWh by 2035 [39]. US DOE National Blueprint target: <$60/kWh at-scale solid-state by 2030 [40]; USABC target $125/kWh [17]. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence: <4 GWh of prototype solid-state cells produced in 2023 — orders of magnitude below Li-ion [40]. Ford's Masias: SSB "very difficult to see … cost-competitive in the near-term" [40]; Solid Power claims a 15–35% pack-level cost advantage over Li-ion (self-reported) [40].
- **Recycling is an open gap:** limited understanding of SSB recyclability vs LIB; direct (reuse/resynthesis) vs indirect routes under research [42]; SSB-specific separations (e.g., β-Li3PS4 dissolution) are immature; DOE-labs paper notes recyclability is claimed but unproven [18].
- **Confidence:** high for Li-ion cost numbers (McKinsey read); medium for solid-state cost projections (model-based, self-reported).

### 7. Open problems and expert disagreements

- **Is all-solid-state needed, or will semi-solid win?** Active disagreement: 2026 is called the "mass production year" for semi-solid (gel/solid-liquid hybrid) batteries, with Chinese experts (SVOLT's Yang Hongxin vs CAS Institute of Physics' Huang Xuejie) publicly differing on whether semi-solid is a bridge or a destination [43]; western commentary likewise treats semi-solid as the bridge technology before full solid-state [44]. The stakes: semi-solid avoids the hardest solid–solid interface problems but forfeits part of the safety/energy argument.
- **Does solid-state actually deliver the promised energy density at cell/pack level?** Targets of >500 Wh/kg and >1000 Wh/L are widely quoted [17][18][15], but modeling of a practical LLZO pouch cell gives only ~272 Wh/kg (~823 Wh/L) — barely above today's 250–270 Wh/kg Li-ion — due to LLZO's density/weight, thick electrolyte and packaging [19]. Randau et al. (2020) benchmarked minimal ASSBs against Li-ion and highlighted how far lab cells lag on interdependent performance metrics [16]. Burton et al. (2024) quantify the zero-lithium-excess trade-off: inhomogeneous plating → CE penalty → excess Li needed → energy density lost [17]. So "solid-state beats Li-ion" is not automatic; it depends on thin separators, lean electrolyte, and anode-free operation — all of which are exactly the hard problems.
- **Anode-free viability.** Anode-free (zero-excess Li) maximizes energy density and simplifies manufacturing but suffers accelerated short-circuiting from local Li depletion [21] and CE losses [17]; mechanistic understanding is still emerging (electro-chemo-mechanics perspective) [20]; seeded nucleation [55/20] and internal-pressure design are mitigation research lines. QuantumScape's anode-free platform is the most prominent commercial bet (T3's track), with zero-pressure claims [45][46].
- **CCD as a metric and "dendrite-free" claims.** Standardization of CCD measurement is itself an open debate [7]; headline "dendrite-free/high-CCD" claims without protocol details are unreliable [9][15].
- **Safety is not automatically guaranteed.** Solid electrolytes remove flammable liquid, but Li-metal SSBs can still release substantial heat in failure scenarios (first thermodynamic models) [41]; safety depends on cell design, not just electrolyte solidity [41][15].
- **Which electrolyte class wins at scale** (sulfides for processability vs oxides for stability vs halides as hybrids) remains open; the reviews explicitly frame the field as unresolved [15][14][19].

## Open problems list

1. Preventing void formation/contact loss at Li|SE interfaces without high stack pressure (creep engineering, lithiophilic interlayers, thin-Li processing) [4][12][51][17].
2. Fully mechanistic, standardized CCD and short-circuit testing; reconciling pore-initiation vs grain-boundary-path models with propagation models [1][7][9].
3. Widening the effective stability window of sulfides vs both Li and high-voltage cathodes (interlayers, coatings, doping) [32][33][34].
4. Manufacturing sulfides outside dry rooms (surface passivation, humid-air processing) or lowering dry-room cost [27][26].
5. Thin (≤50 µm), defect-free, scalable ceramic/halide membranes — the gap between 500 µm press pellets and commercial pouch cells [49][19].
6. Sintering/processing temperature reduction for oxides (sintering aids, reactive/ultrafast sintering) [36][37].
7. Recycling processes designed for SSB-specific materials (sulfide/oxide/halide separations) [42].
8. Anode-free cells: plating homogeneity, CE, and short-circuit suppression at scale [21][17][20].
9. Pack-level integration of stack pressure vs zero-pressure architectures; form-factor development [14][45][49].
10. Whether the >500 Wh/kg promise survives realistic cell/pack modeling (thin electrolyte, lean Li, bipolar stacking) [19][16][17].
