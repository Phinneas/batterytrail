Technically yes, but a car battery will fail quickly in RV house use and won't power your loads reliably. Car batteries are **starting** batteries, built for short high-current bursts, not **deep-cycle** batteries, built for sustained discharge and recharge.

## Starting Batteries vs Deep-Cycle Batteries

The difference is physical, not marketing. It comes down to how the lead plates are built.

**Starting batteries** use many thin, porous plates to maximize surface area. That geometry delivers huge current briefly, typically **400–600 cold cranking amps for 1–2 seconds** to turn an engine over. The alternator then immediately refills what was used. A starting battery is designed to live near full charge and **never drop below about 80% state of charge**.

**Deep-cycle batteries** use fewer, thicker plates with denser active material. They deliver less peak current but survive being drained and refilled repeatedly, to **50% for AGM** and **80% or more for LiFePO4**, over hundreds or thousands of cycles.

Thin plates are the problem. Deep discharge causes lead sulfate to form and shed from the plate surface, and thin plates have very little material to lose before capacity collapses. Thick plates tolerate the same abuse for years.

## What Happens If You Do It Anyway

A car battery running RV house loads, fridge, lights, fan overnight, is doing exactly what its plates can't tolerate.

Expect permanent damage after **10–20 deep discharge cycles.** On a weekend camping schedule, that's a single season. Capacity typically falls to **30–40% of rated within about two months** of regular RV use. A 70Ah car battery that once gave you a working evening now gives you two hours.

The failure is not recoverable. Shed active material sits at the bottom of the case and doesn't go back on the plates. No charger, desulfator, or maintenance routine reverses it.

Two related mistakes worth naming:

- **"Marine/RV" dual-purpose batteries** are a real compromise, not a trick. They handle moderate cycling better than a starting battery but nowhere near a true deep-cycle. Fine for a boat that starts an engine and runs a fish finder; underpowered for boondocking.
- **A car battery as an emergency stopgap** is fine. Getting through one weekend with whatever you have on hand won't hurt anything but that battery. The problem is treating it as the permanent house bank.

## What to Use Instead

Two chemistries make sense for an RV house bank.

**AGM deep-cycle** is the budget option. Sealed, no watering, no venting requirement, and available at any RV dealer or auto parts store in the country. You get 50% usable capacity and 4–7 years of service. The right choice if you mostly camp on shore power and only occasionally go off-grid.

**LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate)** costs more upfront and wins on every other axis. You get 80%+ usable capacity, roughly a third of the weight, and 3,000 to 5,000 cycles at 80% DoD against AGM's 300 to 700 at 50% DoD. For anyone boondocking regularly, the cost per usable amp-hour over the battery's life is lower than AGM.

Whichever you choose, size the bank to your actual loads rather than buying whatever fits the tray. A correctly sized AGM bank beats an undersized lithium one.
