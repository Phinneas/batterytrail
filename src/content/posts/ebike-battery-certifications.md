Two certifications matter most for ebike battery buying: UL 2849 tests the entire electrical system, meaning the battery, charger, and motor controller together, while UL 2271 tests the battery pack by itself. If you're buying in New York City, only products certified to one or both can legally be sold, and if you're buying for use in Europe, the certification framework is entirely different, built around CE marking and EN 15194 instead of UL standards.

## UL 2849 vs. UL 2271: What Each One Actually Tests

UL 2849, the Standard for Electrical Systems for eBikes, evaluates the complete electrical drive train as one integrated system: the battery pack, the battery management system, the motor controller, the charger, and the wiring harness connecting them. Testing covers overcharging, short circuits, crush impacts, thermal runaway initiation, water exposure, and the vibration an ebike experiences during normal riding. A system only earns certification if it survives every test without fire, explosion, or dangerous electrical discharge.

UL 2271 is narrower. It certifies the lithium-ion battery pack on its own, testing resistance to overcharging, short circuits, extreme temperatures, impact, and crushing. An ebike can carry a UL 2271-certified battery without the rest of the electrical system being certified to UL 2849, which is an important distinction: a certified battery inside an uncertified charging and control system is not the same level of assurance as a fully certified UL 2849 system. UL 2849 was published in January 2020 and is now recognized as an Approved American National Standard (ANSI) as well as a National Standard of Canada.

## Why New York City Requires These Specific Certifications

NYC's Local Law 39, effective September 16, 2023, bars the sale, lease, or rental of any ebike not certified to UL 2849, any standalone battery not certified to UL 2271, and any e-scooter not certified to UL 2272. The law followed a sharp rise in lithium-ion battery fires: FDNY responded to 268 such fires in 2023 alone, causing 18 deaths and 150 injuries. In the first full year after enforcement began, fire deaths in comparable nine-month periods dropped from 14 to 3.

Local Law 95, effective January 26, 2026, extended the requirement from point of sale to point of use for delivery workers specifically. Any powered bicycle operated by a contracted delivery worker on behalf of a delivery service now must meet the same certification standards, with responsibility for compliance falling on the delivery platform rather than the individual rider. Look for the UL 2849 mark on the frame or head tube, and the UL 2271 mark on the battery itself. NYC's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection publishes the official list of accepted testing labs, which currently includes UL Solutions, SGS, Intertek, and TÜV SÜD.

## CE Marking and EN 15194: The European Equivalent

Europe doesn't use UL standards at all. Ebikes sold in the EU need CE marking, which requires compliance with EN 15194, the harmonized standard for electrically power-assisted cycles (EPACs). EN 15194 caps continuous motor power at 250W and requires assistance to cut off at 25 km/h, alongside electrical safety and frame strength requirements.

Battery safety under EN 15194 changed in 2023. The Netherlands formally objected that the standard's original battery references, based on the more general EN 62133-2, didn't adequately address ebike-specific fire risk. The European Commission partially withdrew harmonization for the battery-related sections, and the standard was updated to reference EN 50604-1 instead, a much more detailed lithium battery standard covering crushing, water immersion, sun exposure, and fire resistance testing. The current standard is published as EN 15194:2017+A1:2023, and EN 50604-1 compliance is now effectively mandatory to achieve CE marking and presumption of conformity for the battery.

One detail worth flagging: for many EPACs, CE marking is based on manufacturer self-declaration against these harmonized standards rather than mandatory third-party notified body testing. A CE mark confirms the manufacturer's compliance declaration, not automatically an independent lab's verification, unless higher-risk components trigger notified body involvement.

## UN 38.3: The Certification That Has Nothing to Do With Riding Safety

UN 38.3 shows up on nearly every ebike battery listing, and it's worth understanding what it isn't. It's a transport safety standard, required for any lithium battery shipped by air, sea, rail, or road, regardless of what device it powers. The testing simulates altitude pressure, extreme temperature cycling, vibration, and short circuit conditions a battery might face during shipping, not during years of riding and charging. A UN 38.3 pass is necessary for a battery to legally reach you at all, but it says nothing about whether the finished ebike's electrical system is safe to use, which is what UL 2849 and EN 15194 are built to verify.

## How to Check for These Marks Before You Buy

Check the frame, head tube, or battery casing directly for a certification mark and listing number, not just marketing language claiming "UL certified" or "CE certified." A real UL listing is searchable in UL's own product database. For NYC purchases specifically, cross-reference the seller against DCWP's list of accepted testing labs. If a listing shows a certification logo with no listing number or lab name attached, treat it as unverified.

## Ebike Certification Comparison

| Certification | What it tests | Where it's required |
|---|---|---|
| UL 2849 | Full electrical system: battery, charger, motor controller, wiring | Legally required in NYC (Local Law 39) |
| UL 2271 | Battery pack alone | Legally required in NYC for standalone batteries |
| CE / EN 15194 | Complete EPAC system, 250W/25 km/h limits | Required for sale in the EU |
| EN 50604-1 | Lithium battery safety within EN 15194 | Required for CE marking since 2023 update |
| UN 38.3 | Transport safety only | Required for shipping any lithium battery |
